The Diagnosis That Became a PDF

A patient spends a night in a sleep lab. The study finds severe obstructive sleep apnea, and the result comes back to the chart the way it often does: as a scanned report. A human can open it. No rule, registry, or pre-op checklist can. Two years later that patient is scheduled for surgery, and the system that should warn anesthesia cannot see a diagnosis it is already holding. This project is about turning a sleep study from a document into data.

The 30-second version
1

Sleep apnea matters in the operating room

Anesthesia and post-op opioids relax the airway further. A patient with severe obstructive sleep apnea needs a different plan: their CPAP at bedside, careful opioid dosing, closer monitoring.

2

Most cases are never diagnosed

So pre-op teams screen with a short questionnaire, STOP-Bang. It catches most high-risk patients, but it flags many who turn out fine, and it misses some.

3

The diagnosed ones should be the easy part

They already had the gold-standard test. But when the result lives in a scanned PDF, the chart cannot act on it. They get screened like strangers, and a CPAP user who no longer snores can score low.

4

The fix is an interface, not an algorithm

Send the key numbers as coded, discrete results. Then one simple rule can do what the questionnaire can't: recognize every patient whose diagnosis is already on file.

04 — What becomes possible

A query that returns zero rows today

Once the index is a discrete result, the question “who is scheduled for surgery in the next two weeks with known moderate-to-severe sleep apnea?” becomes a report anyone can run, and the same logic can drive a pre-op flag. Against PDF-only results, the identical query returns nothing, because there is nothing to query.

Pre-op OSA worklist
Clarity-style SQL · illustrative · table, column and LOINC-mapping names vary by build
-- Patients with surgery in the next 14 days and a prior obstructive AHI of 15 or more
SELECT  pat.PAT_MRN_ID,
        orc.SURGERY_DATE,
        res.ORD_NUM_VALUE        AS oahi,
        res.RESULT_TIME          AS study_date,
        CASE WHEN res.ORD_NUM_VALUE >= 30 THEN 'SEVERE' ELSE 'MODERATE' END AS severity
FROM    OR_CASE orc
JOIN    PATIENT pat          ON pat.PAT_ID = orc.PAT_ID
JOIN    ORDER_PROC op        ON op.PAT_ID = orc.PAT_ID
JOIN    ORDER_RESULTS res    ON res.ORDER_PROC_ID = op.ORDER_PROC_ID
JOIN    COMPONENT_LOINC_MAP m ON m.COMPONENT_ID = res.COMPONENT_ID   -- local mapping; name varies
WHERE   m.LOINC_CODE = '90563-8'                                      -- obstructive AHI
  AND   res.ORD_NUM_VALUE >= 15
  AND   res.RESULT_TIME >= DATEADD(year, -5, GETDATE())
  AND   orc.SURGERY_DATE BETWEEN GETDATE() AND DATEADD(day, 14, GETDATE())
ORDER BY orc.SURGERY_DATE;

-- Against PDF-only results: 0 rows. The AHI lives inside a scanned image.
The pre-op flag, step by step
automation moves information · humans make the plan
1 · TRIGGER

Pre-op visit or case scheduled

The check runs once per surgical episode, at the point the team is already reviewing the chart.

2 · KNOWN?

Discrete AHI ≥ 15 in the last 5 years

If yes: flag as known OSA, prompt to confirm CPAP use and bring the device. No questionnaire needed.

3 · SCREEN

Otherwise, STOP-Bang at the chosen cutoff

Flag as possible OSA. Four of the eight items (blood pressure history, BMI, age, sex) can often prefill from structured data.

4 · HUMAN DECISION

Anesthesia sets the plan

Monitoring level, opioid strategy, sleep-medicine referral, whether to proceed. The flag informs; it does not decide.

05 — What this can't do

Screening is not diagnosis, and flags are not free

Move the cutoff in the simulator and the tradeoff is plain: at the standard cutoff, more than half of all patients get flagged and most of them do not have significant sleep apnea. Raise it and the flags shrink, but real cases slip through. Structuring the result does not remove that tradeoff for undiagnosed patients. It removes it for the ones already diagnosed.

Limits worth saying out loud

  • STOP-Bang is a screen. High sensitivity, low specificity at the usual cutoff. A flag means “consider,” never “has”
  • It asks about symptoms, so treated patients can under-score. That is exactly the group a structured result catches
  • The sex item has been questioned in the literature as a reason women may be under-screened. Worth monitoring flag rates by sex
  • A 5-year lookback is a design choice. Weight change or surgery can change severity; the flag should show the study date
  • Home sleep tests report differently from in-lab studies. The interface has to handle both, or the rule quietly misses one

Governance, same as every alert

  • Alert fatigue is the failure mode. A pre-op team drowning in flags learns to click through them
  • Split the flag: “known OSA, bring CPAP” is high-value and low-volume. “Possible OSA” is high-volume and needs its own threshold
  • Measure it: flag volume, action rate, and how many known-OSA patients still arrive without their device
  • Own the mapping table. A local code that silently stops mapping after a sleep-system upgrade breaks the rule with no error
  • Clinical leadership owns the cutoff. The analyst owns making the tradeoff visible
06 — The business case

What a result that can be read is worth

Lead with capacity and safety, dollars second. Every repeat sleep study ordered for an already-diagnosed patient is a sleep-lab night taken from someone on the waitlist, and every known patient who reaches the OR unflagged is avoidable risk.

07 — Why this project

The same pattern, one layer down

The epilepsy unit project found a decision running a day behind its own data. This one finds a decision that can't see its data at all, because the data arrived as a picture. The toolkit is the portfolio's: clinical context from the neurology work, alert governance from the neuro-ICU pipeline, and the interface literacy from the Epic analyst lab, aimed at a gap that is common, fixable, and unglamorous.

Demonstrated here

  • Interface design: HL7 v2 ORU and FHIR R4 for the same result, with LOINC coding and an honest local-code gap
  • Data-to-decision thinking: tracing why a known diagnosis fails to reach the OR
  • Interface troubleshooting: a vendor upgrade that silently breaks a mapping, and the volume monitor that catches it
  • Reporting: a Clarity-style worklist query that only works once the data is discrete
  • Alert governance: splitting a high-value flag from a high-volume one, with metrics
  • Capacity framing: sleep-lab nights and nursing hours before dollars

Evidence notes
  • LOINC codes shown are from the LOINC Polysomnography panel (90568-7): loinc.org/90568-7, including 90563-8 obstructive AHI, 90567-9 oxygen desaturation index, 90566-1 respiratory disturbance index. Sleep efficiency is shown on a local code deliberately.
  • STOP-Bang performance: high sensitivity for moderate and severe OSA at a cutoff of 3 with low specificity; higher cutoffs trade sensitivity for specificity (e.g. Nagappa et al., 2015 meta-analysis; Chung et al., alternative scoring models, J Clin Sleep Med). The simulated population is tuned to reproduce that shape, not any specific study's numbers.
  • “Most moderate-to-severe OSA is undiagnosed” reflects widely cited population estimates; the simulator's 25% diagnosed share is an assumption.
  • All patients, messages, and financial inputs are synthetic or illustrative. No PHI. Not an institutional projection.

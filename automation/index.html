The Bot That Worked Too Fast

A person who makes a mistake makes it once and notices. A software bot makes it 4,000 times before lunch, signed with an account nobody can quite explain. This is a control tower for five bots doing real hospital paperwork: flip the safety settings on or off, run a 24-hour shift, and read what happened. The guardrails you skip are the incidents you get.

Hospitals are hiring software robots

Not physical robots — programs that do the paperwork: filing insurance authorizations, cleaning up patient records, sorting inbox messages. The time savings are real and large.

A bot's mistake doesn't happen once

A person who fills a form wrong does it once and notices. A bot configured wrong does it four thousand times before lunch — and nobody finds out until patients start calling.

Six settings decide whether that happens

Speed limits, running hours, what to do when unsure, what data it can touch, how to stop it, and who owns it. All six are optional. Skipping them makes the bot faster — that's why they get skipped.

01 — The fleet

Five bots, five very different risk profiles

Each bot does a real hospital back-office job. What makes them different isn't difficulty — it's how bad it is when they're wrong. A bot that tidies an address field and a bot that files insurance authorizations fail in completely different ways, so they shouldn't get the same rules.

03 — The identity problem

A bot that reads charts is an account that reads charts

Every bot logs into systems using an account, just like a person does. That account can read patient records. So it needs the same things a staff account needs — limited access, rotating passwords, an owner — plus one more: when something goes wrong, "the bot did it" has to point to a real person.

What every bot identity needs

  • A named human owner — not a team, not a vendor. Someone who can answer for its actions and approve its scope changes
  • Least-privilege scope — a bot that reads demographics does not need chart write access, and "we'll tighten it later" is a sentence that never gets finished
  • Credential rotation — service account passwords that never expire are the ones that end up in a script in a shared drive
  • Distinguishable audit trail — bot actions must be separable from human actions in the log, or every investigation starts with a week of disambiguation
  • A documented purpose — an account whose reason for existing is undocumented is an account nobody will dare to disable

How it actually goes wrong

  • Scope creep by convenience — the bot needed one more permission for one more task, five times, and now it can do anything
  • The orphaned bot — its owner left the organization; nobody knows what it does, so nobody turns it off, and it runs for years
  • Shared credentials — two bots on one service account means neither one's actions can be attributed to it
  • The human-shaped bot — running automation under a real employee's login, which makes the audit trail actively false rather than merely unclear
  • Break-the-glass at scale — a bot with emergency-access rights that uses them routinely, turning an exception into a habit
Why this is a clinical systems problem, not just an IT one: in a health system every one of these accounts touches PHI. A bot with unnecessary chart access is a HIPAA minimum-necessary finding that runs continuously, at machine speed, with no one watching — and the day it's discovered, the question asked will be "who approved this scope," not "which vendor built it."
04 — The decision framework

Which rung is this task allowed to occupy?

"Can we automate this" is almost always yes, and it's the wrong question. The real one is how much the machine gets to decide alone — which depends on whether the action can be undone and how badly it can hurt someone. Most hospital automation belongs on rungs 1 and 2. Trouble starts when a bot quietly climbs a rung nobody approved.

0

Machine records, human does

No autonomy. Correct for anything rare, ambiguous, or unrecoverable — and an honest answer more often than automation programs like to admit.

1

Machine flags, human decides

The bot surfaces candidates; a qualified human owns the decision. All of the throughput, none of the delegated judgment. Where clinical-facing automation belongs.

2

Machine acts on reversible, low-stakes work

Autonomy where a wrong action costs minutes and can be undone. Most RPA value lives here — and so does most RPA risk, because "low stakes" times ten thousand executions stops being low stakes.

3

Machine acts, human can veto in a window

Requires a veto window that someone is genuinely watching. Usually a design smell when proposed for anything irreversible.

4

Fully autonomous

In direct patient care, reserved for regulated closed-loop devices with their own safety engineering — not for workflow automation, and never as an efficiency shortcut.

05 — Why this project

Automation is an operations problem wearing a technology costume

Health systems are deploying bots quickly because the ROI is real and immediate — the hours returned in the simulator above are not exaggerated. What lags behind is the governance: who owns each bot, what it's scoped to touch, how fast it's allowed to move, and what stops it at 3 a.m. This project builds the control layer that should exist before the fleet does, and it connects directly to the identity and access work that governs every non-human actor in a clinical environment.

Demonstrated here

  • Blast-radius thinking — rate limits, execution windows, and scope as the primary risk controls for automation
  • Service identity design — ownership, least privilege, rotation, and distinguishable audit attribution
  • Exception handling as a control — the human queue is what keeps a bot on rung 2 instead of silently climbing to rung 3
  • ROI framed honestly — hours returned stated alongside the risk that produced them
  • Autonomy governance — reversibility × harm as an explicit, defensible decision rather than a default

Interview talking points

  • "Automation risk is the probability of a mistake multiplied by the speed it replicates — which is why rate limiting is a safety control, not a performance setting"
  • "A bot that reads charts is an account that reads charts: owner, scope, rotation, and an audit trail that separates it from humans"
  • "The exception queue is the difference between rung 2 and rung 3, and most bots climb that rung without anyone deciding to let them"
  • "Every bulk operation against interfaced data is an interface event, whether its owner thinks of it that way or not"
  • "I'd rather ship a slower bot with a kill switch than a fast one whose owner left the company"

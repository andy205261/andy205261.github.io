The Seizure Nobody Can See

In the neuro ICU, some patients are seizing with no outward movement at all — nothing to see, and every undetected hour costs brain tissue. A monitor can find it. That part is solved. Getting the alert to the right clinician without burying the unit in false alarms is the unsolved part — and it's a systems problem, not a math problem. This is that system.

The 30-second version
1

Some seizures are invisible

In the neuro ICU, a patient can be seizing in their brain with no shaking, no movement, nothing to see. It only shows up on a brain-wave monitor — and every undetected hour causes more damage.

2

Software can spot them. That's the easy part.

Detection algorithms already exist and work reasonably well. The hard part is everything after: getting the right person told, fast, without crying wolf.

3

Every alert setting has a victim

Set it too sensitive and nurses get buried in false alarms until they ignore all of them. Set it too strict and you miss the subtle seizures you bought the machine to find. There is no setting that avoids both.

4

Someone has to choose the number

That choice belongs to the hospital, not the vendor's default. This project puts the tradeoff on one screen so a clinician, a nurse manager, and an IT analyst can look at the same picture and decide together.

01 — The signal

What the detector actually sees

Four channels of simulated brain activity. Normal background looks irregular and small. A seizure looks like a rhythmic wave that builds — that's the pattern the software scores. Movement — chewing, turning the patient, a loose electrode — can look uncomfortably similar, and that resemblance is the entire reason false alarms exist.

04 — The business case

What the right threshold is worth

The clinical argument for tuning an alert is easy to make and easy to ignore. The financial one is harder to ignore. Two costs move when the threshold moves: ICU days from seizures caught late, and nursing hours burned on false alarms. Both are measurable, and the second one is almost never measured.

05 — Routing

An alert with no owner is not an alert

An alert nobody receives is the same as no alert at all. Here is who gets told, when, and what happens if they don't respond.

T0 · 0s
Bedside display + unit dashboard
Detection surfaces on the bedside cEEG display and the neuro ICU dashboard immediately. Passive, non-interruptive — the nurse who is already in the room sees it without being paged.
T1 · 0s
Assigned RN — mobile push
Interruptive push to the assigned nurse's device via the clinical communication platform, with bed, pattern class, and duration. Requires acknowledgment.
T2 · 3 min
Unacknowledged → charge RN + neuro fellow
No acknowledgment in three minutes means the nurse is in a code, in isolation gear, or the message failed. The system does not wait to find out which — it widens.
T3 · 10 min
Sustained pattern → neurointensivist attending
A pattern persisting past ten minutes meets the operational definition of status epilepticus. This is no longer a monitoring event; it is a treatment decision that requires an attending.
Always
Every event → reviewable record
Each detection — acted on, dismissed, or false — writes to a review queue with the raw epoch attached. This is the substrate for weekly tuning, quality review, and the false-positive rate that governs the threshold above. No event disappears.
06 — Automation governance

How much should the machine be allowed to decide?

The real question is never "can we automate this" — it usually can be. It's how much the machine is allowed to decide on its own. The answer depends on two things: can the action be undone, and how badly can it hurt someone.

0

Human does it, machine records

No autonomy. The system observes and documents. Safe, and often the correct answer for anything rare, ambiguous, or unrecoverable.

e.g. manual EEG review by a neurophysiologist; automation only stores and displays
1

Machine flags, human decides

This is where the cEEG detector belongs. The algorithm surfaces a candidate; a qualified human confirms the pattern and owns the treatment decision. All the sensitivity of automation, none of the delegated judgment.

e.g. seizure detection alert → clinician reviews the epoch → treats or dismisses
2

Machine acts on low-stakes, reversible tasks

Autonomy granted where a wrong action costs minutes, not harm, and can be undone. Most RPA value in a health system lives here — and so does most RPA risk, because a bot at scale makes the same mistake thousands of times before anyone notices.

e.g. bot files prior-auth submissions, reconciles worklists, populates registry data
3

Machine acts, human can veto in a window

Action proceeds unless interrupted. Requires the veto window to be real — visible to someone who is actually watching, with enough time to intervene. Rare in clinical settings, and usually a design smell when proposed for anything irreversible.

e.g. auto-scheduled follow-up that a scheduler can cancel before it sends
4

Machine acts autonomously

No human in the loop. In direct patient care this rung is reserved for closed-loop devices under device regulation and their own safety engineering — not for workflow automation, and never as an efficiency shortcut.

e.g. closed-loop insulin delivery — a regulated device, not an IT project
The identity question nobody asks early enough: a bot that reads charts is an account that reads charts. It needs a service identity, scoped least-privilege access, credential rotation, and an audit trail that distinguishes its actions from a human's. When an automated action turns out to be wrong, "who did this" must have an answer — and "the bot" is only an acceptable answer if the bot has an owner, a scope, and a log.
07 — What actually breaks

Failure modes worth designing against

Silent death of the pipeline

  • Electrode impedance drift — signal quality degrades over hours; the detector keeps scoring garbage and reports nothing wrong
  • Amplifier disconnect — no data is not a low score, but a naive dashboard renders both as "quiet"
  • The fix: monitor signal quality and heartbeat, not just detections — absence of alerts must be provably different from absence of monitoring

Alert fatigue as a safety failure

  • Above roughly 90% override rates, an alert stops protecting anyone and starts training clinicians to dismiss all alerts
  • A threshold set for maximum sensitivity with no tuning review is how a unit arrives there in three weeks
  • The fix: treat alert burden per nurse per shift as a governed metric with a scheduled review, not a one-time config

Delivery gaps

  • Assigned-nurse mapping goes stale at shift change and alerts route to whoever was on at 07:00
  • Device wifi roaming drops the push; nobody learns the message was never delivered
  • The fix: acknowledgment-based escalation — undelivered and unacknowledged must behave identically, because to the patient they are identical

Documentation drift

  • The detection exists in the vendor system; the chart shows nothing, so the clinical record and the monitoring record diverge
  • Quality review later cannot reconstruct what was known and when
  • The fix: every reviewed event writes a discrete, timestamped record into the chart — the EHR is the legal narrative, not the vendor console
08 — Why this project

The question after the algorithm works

Project 01 built seizure detection from EEG features and asked whether the model could find the pattern. This project asks the harder question that determines whether any of that reaches a patient: who gets told, how fast, at what false-alarm cost, with which human holding the decision — and who is accountable when the automation is wrong. That question is the job description of a clinical systems analyst working on automation, and it is the same question whether the pipeline carries a seizure detector, an RPA bot, or a sepsis model.

Demonstrated here

  • Device-to-EHR integration — the multi-hop pipeline, its owners, and its silent failure points
  • Alert governance with real math — sensitivity, PPV, alert burden, and the tradeoff made explicit for decision-makers
  • Escalation design — acknowledgment-driven routing with time-bound widening
  • Automation autonomy framework — reversibility and harm as the axes that set the rung
  • Service identity for bots — least privilege and attribution for non-human actors

Interview talking points

  • "Absence of alerts has to be provably different from absence of monitoring"

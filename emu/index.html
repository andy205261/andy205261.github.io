The Ward Where Patients Wait to Have a Seizure

An epilepsy monitoring unit admits patients for days, tapers their medication on purpose, and waits — because the only way to find out where a seizure starts is to catch one on camera. Beds are few, the waitlist runs months, and every day a patient stays after the answer is already in the data is a day the next patient spends waiting at home, still seizing. This project is about the one decision that governs the whole ward: do we have enough yet?

The 30-second version
1

Some epilepsy can be cured by surgery

If seizures always start from one small spot in the brain, a surgeon can remove or ablate it. But first someone has to prove exactly where that spot is.

2

Proving it means catching seizures on camera

The patient is admitted, wired for continuous EEG and video, and their medication is reduced deliberately so seizures happen. The team needs several typical events to localize with confidence.

3

Nobody knows when they'll have them

Could be day two. Could be day seven. So the stay ends not on a schedule but on a judgment call, repeated every morning: do we have enough data yet?

4

And that call is made on yesterday's information

If events are reviewed the next day, discharge happens a day late — every time. Six beds, a months-long waitlist, and a full day of capacity leaking out of each admission.

02 — The bottleneck

Where the day actually goes

Nothing in this pathway is technically hard. The delay is structural — a chain of handoffs where each link is reasonable and the sum is a lost day per admission.

How the day disappears

  • Events happen overnight — seizures cluster at night and during sleep-deprivation protocols, which is exactly when the fewest people are watching
  • Review is batched — a tech works through hours of video-EEG, often the following morning, marking which events are typical
  • The epileptologist rounds once — if review isn't finished before rounds, the decision waits a full day
  • Discharge has its own tail — electrode removal, orders, transport, education. Deciding at 2pm often means leaving tomorrow anyway

What automation can and can't do

  • Can: flag candidate events as they occur, so the review queue is minutes of clips instead of hours of recording
  • Can: surface a running count against the target, visible on rounds — "3 typical events captured, target met"
  • Can: alert staff in real time when an event begins, which is a safety function before it is an efficiency one
  • Cannot: decide whether an event is typical for that patient — that is the clinical judgment the entire admission exists to produce
  • Cannot: localize the seizure onset zone. Detection narrows where humans look; it does not replace them
The design principle, same as everywhere else in this portfolio: the automation moves information to the humans faster. It does not make the call. A detector that flags 40 candidate events and lets a tech confirm 3 in ten minutes has done its entire job — and it is the difference between discharging today and discharging tomorrow.
03 — The part that isn't about throughput

These patients are seizing on purpose

It is worth saying plainly: an EMU deliberately provokes seizures in people who have epilepsy, by reducing the medication that was protecting them. That is medically justified — it is the only way to get the recording that makes a curative surgery possible — and it means the unit carries risks a normal ward does not.

What the unit is guarding against

  • Status epilepticus — a seizure that doesn't stop on its own. Provoked by the same taper that makes the admission work
  • Falls and injury — a patient mid-seizure, tethered to electrodes, in an unfamiliar room
  • Post-ictal confusion — disoriented patients attempting to get up, pull leads, or leave
  • Unwitnessed events — the worst case is an event nobody sees in real time

Why real-time detection is a safety control first

  • An event flagged as it happens brings staff to the room — that is the primary purpose, and throughput is a byproduct
  • Alert fatigue applies here too: a detector tuned too sensitively gets muted, and a muted detector protects nobody
  • Same governance discipline as every other alert: measure the false-positive rate, review it on a schedule, let clinical leadership own the threshold
  • The efficiency case and the safety case want the same thing — which is the rare situation where nobody has to argue
04 — The business case

What one day per admission is worth

EMU beds are scarce, the waitlist is long, and the unit feeds a surgical pathway. That combination means length of stay converts almost directly into additional patients treated — and unlike most efficiency projects, the clinical argument and the financial one point the same direction.

How to present this

—

Why this case is unusually easy to make: most throughput projects have to argue that faster is safe. Here faster is safer — real-time event detection brings staff to a seizing patient sooner, and shortening a deliberately-tapered admission reduces the window in which a provoked seizure can go wrong. And epilepsy surgery, for the right patient, is one of the few genuinely curative procedures in neurology. Every additional patient who reaches it is not a throughput statistic.
05 — Why this project

A neurology ward that runs on a capacity problem

This project sits at the intersection the rest of the portfolio has been circling: a genuinely neurological problem, solved with clinical automation, justified by a capacity argument an operating committee already understands. The EEG expertise comes from the seizure detection work; the alert governance comes from the neuro-ICU pipeline; the throughput framing comes from the CAR-T capacity model. It is the same analyst toolkit, aimed at a ward where scarcity is the defining constraint.

Demonstrated here

  • Workflow bottleneck analysis — identifying that the delay is in review timing, not clinical decision-making
  • Automation scoped correctly — narrow the review queue, never decide typicality
  • Safety-first framing — real-time detection justified as a patient-safety control before an efficiency one
  • Capacity economics — LOS converted into admissions, surgical candidates, and waitlist movement
  • Service-line literacy — understanding that the EMU exists to feed a surgical pathway, not as an end in itself

Interview talking points

  • "The bottleneck wasn't the decision — it was that the decision ran a day behind the data that would have made it"
  • "Detection narrows where humans look. It never decides whether an event is typical — that judgment is the entire reason for the admission"
  • "In a scarce unit feeding a curative surgery, a day of length of stay is a patient who stays on the waitlist"
  • "This is the rare case where the safety argument and the throughput argument want the identical intervention"

Twenty-Two Seconds of Warning

A seizure doesn't start when the shaking starts. The brain changes first — quietly, in ways a person can't feel and an eye can't see on the tracing. This project pulls 25 measurements per second of brain activity and trains a model to notice that change, then shows exactly which measurement made it suspicious. Not a black box: every prediction explains itself.

The 30-second version
1

Brain waves are just a squiggly line

An EEG records electrical activity from the scalp. To a computer it's a stream of numbers — thousands per second, and no obvious way to tell "fine" from "about to seize."

2

So you describe the squiggle with numbers

Each one-second slice gets measured 25 ways: how fast it wiggles, how jagged, how predictable, how much energy at each frequency. That turns a picture into a row of data a model can learn from.

3

The change starts before the seizure does

In the pre-ictal window — the minute or so before onset — those numbers begin drifting. Not enough for a person to notice. Enough for a model trained on 11,500 examples.

4

And it has to show its work

A model that says "seizure, 94% confident" and can't say why is useless in a hospital. Every prediction here breaks down which measurements pushed it that way — and by how much.

02 — Feature extraction

How you turn a squiggle into 25 numbers

This is the part that decides whether the model can learn anything at all. A raw one-second slice is 178 meaningless values; these measurements are what make it describable. The bars below move live with the signal above — watch which ones react first.

What each family measures

  • Band powers — how much energy sits in each frequency range (delta through gamma). Seizures shift energy dramatically toward specific bands
  • Line length — total distance the line travels in a second. A jagged, high-amplitude second travels much further than a calm one
  • Hjorth parameters — activity, mobility, complexity: three classic descriptors of how a signal wiggles and how that wiggle changes
  • Spectral entropy — how predictable the frequency content is. Normal brain activity is messy; seizures are rhythmic, so entropy drops

Why not just feed in raw signal

  • Raw EEG is high-dimensional and noisy — a model trained directly on it needs far more data than most clinical datasets contain
  • Engineered features carry clinical meaning, which is what makes the explanation in the next section possible at all
  • A neurologist recognizes "spectral entropy dropped" as a real phenomenon. "Weight in layer 3 changed" is not something anyone can act on
  • Feature-based models train in seconds on a laptop and can be audited line by line — both of which matter more in healthcare than a marginal accuracy gain
03 — Explainability

Why this section is the point of the whole project: a model that can't explain itself cannot be deployed in a hospital, cannot be debugged when it drifts, and cannot be defended when it's wrong. Explainability isn't a nice-to-have bolted on at the end — it's what determines whether the work leaves the notebook. The moment a neurologist can look at an attribution and say "yes, that's what I'd have looked at too" is the moment the model becomes usable.

What worked

  • Ensemble methods dominated — Random Forest and Gradient Boosting both handled the non-linear feature interactions that linear models missed entirely
  • Line length and spectral entropy carried the most signal, which matches what the clinical literature reports — a good sign the model learned physiology, not dataset artifacts
  • The pre-ictal window is real and detectable — features begin drifting measurably before onset, which is what makes early warning possible at all

What I'd say in an interview

  • "Accuracy on a clean Kaggle dataset is the easy part — the honest question is how it behaves on messy clinical EEG full of movement artifact"
  • "I chose engineered features over deep learning specifically because a neurologist can audit them, and that tradeoff is correct in healthcare"
  • "22 seconds sounds small until you consider what it enables: getting a patient to a safe position, or starting the clock on a treatment decision"
  • "This project is where I learned that a model's accuracy and a model's usability are two separate problems — the second one is why I built the alerting pipeline next"
05 — What came next

The question this project couldn't answer

This model finds seizures. It cannot tell you who gets told, how fast, at what false-alarm cost, or who is accountable when it's wrong — and those turn out to be the questions that decide whether any of it reaches a patient. That gap is exactly what the neurocritical care pipeline was built to address: the same signal, followed all the way from the electrode to a clinician who has to act on it.

The through-line across this portfolio: a model that detects seizures (this project) is worthless without a pipeline that delivers the alert without drowning the unit in false alarms (Project 02), which depends on the same clinical systems discipline that keeps lab results filing into charts at 7am (Project 03), governed by the same rules that decide how much any automation gets to decide alone (Project 04). Detection is the first hop, not the destination.

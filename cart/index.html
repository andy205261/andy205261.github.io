The Patient Who Couldn't Write a Sentence

A cancer therapy that sometimes attacks the brain. A patient lucid at noon, confused by evening, seizing by morning. The warning sign is quiet — it shows up when someone can no longer write a simple sentence. This project catches that signal 16 hours earlier by watching the direction a score is moving instead of waiting for it to hit a bad number, and then puts a number on what those 16 hours are worth.

The 30-second version
1

A cancer treatment that can attack the brain

CAR-T is a powerful cell therapy for blood cancers. In some patients it triggers brain swelling and confusion. Caught early, it's treatable with steroids. Caught late, the patient ends up in the ICU — or seizing.

2

Nurses already check for it every 8 hours

A simple 3-minute test scored out of 10: what year is it, name these objects, write this sentence. The problem isn't collecting the data — it's that nobody can see the direction it's moving.

3

Watching the direction buys 16 hours

Most alerts wait for the score to hit a bad number. Watching for a falling score instead catches the same patient 16 hours sooner — while treatment still works and the ICU is still avoidable.

4

And 16 hours is worth real money

Fewer ICU days, shorter stays, and — in a cancer program running at full capacity — beds that open up for the next patient waiting for treatment. Better care and better economics point the same direction.

Why handwriting is the canary: writing a sentence requires language, motor planning, and sustained attention at once — so it degrades before orientation does. A patient who still knows the year and the hospital may already be unable to produce a legible sentence. That is the window this whole system exists to catch, and it is measured in hours.

16 hours earlier, while the patient can still hold a conversation and the score is only 8. Two checks in a row moving the wrong way is a signal; one number by itself is not. This is the window where steroids work and the ICU gets avoided.

And the cost of the trend rule, visible on the same board: switch to trend-aware and advance to hour 32 — Bed 05 fires too. That patient had a transient two-point dip after sedation and recovered on their own. This is not a flaw in the demonstration; it is the actual tradeoff. Trend detection buys 16 hours on the patient who is truly declining and costs you an alert on the patient who is not, and the delta threshold that governs that ratio is a number the cell therapy team has to choose and revisit — not a setting an analyst picks alone.
The whole insight in one line: 10 → 9 → 7 is a trajectory; 7 alone is a number. Clinical systems overwhelmingly alert on thresholds because thresholds are easy to configure — and trajectories are what clinicians actually reason about at the bedside. Encoding the trajectory is the entire value add here, and it required no new data, no new device, and no new documentation burden. The nurses were already collecting this.
03 — Routing

What happens after the alert fires

An alert that goes nowhere is a dashboard nobody watches. Here is who gets told, in what order, and what each person is expected to do.

Δ detected
Bedside RN — verify and repeat
The first action is confirmation, not escalation: repeat the ICE assessment and rule out the reversible mimics — sedating medications, sleep deprivation, hypoglycemia, infection. Two-nurse confirmation protects the alert's credibility.
Confirmed
Cell therapy APP + primary team
Structured notification carrying the trajectory, not just the current score — the last three assessments, the delta, and hours since infusion. Time from infusion is diagnostic context: ICANS has a characteristic onset window.
Grade 2+
Attending + neurology consult + cEEG evaluation
Corticosteroid decision point. Neurology consult and consideration of continuous EEG to detect non-convulsive seizure activity — the same cEEG pipeline modeled in the neurocritical care project. Two of the highest-stakes neuro monitoring problems in the hospital converge here.
Any grade
Structured record + program registry
Every assessment, alert, and disposition writes a discrete record. This feeds required cell-therapy program reporting (FACT accreditation, REMS obligations, registry submission) — documentation the program already owes, produced as a byproduct rather than a separate chart-abstraction job.

On honesty in ROI modeling: every input above is adjustable because every input is an assumption, and a business case that hides its assumptions is a sales pitch. The incidence and progression ranges are drawn from published CAR-T neurotoxicity literature; the cost and margin figures are deliberately generic placeholders, not Stanford's actuals. The defensible claim is not a dollar figure — it is the structure: earlier detection reduces severe progression, severe progression drives ICU and length-of-stay, and in a capacity-constrained service, length-of-stay converts directly into forgone treatment slots. Present the model, let finance supply the real inputs.
05 — Governance

The machine never decides

This system sits deliberately on rung 1 of the autonomy ladder: it surfaces a trajectory, and a qualified human decides what it means. That placement is not timidity — it is the only defensible position for a signal whose differential includes medication effect, infection, metabolic derangement, and sleep, all of which require a clinician at the bedside to sort out.

What the automation does

  • Captures ICE assessments as discrete, timestamped data instead of free text buried in notes
  • Computes trajectory — deltas across the last two and three assessments, plus hours since infusion
  • Surfaces a confirmed decline to the right role with the trend attached, not just the current value
  • Records every assessment and disposition for program reporting and threshold review

What stays human

  • The assessment itself — a nurse asking a patient to write a sentence is not automatable, and shouldn't be
  • Differentiating the mimics — sedation, infection, hypoglycemia, and exhaustion all look like early ICANS on a score
  • The grading and treatment decision — steroids, ICU transfer, and neurology consult are clinical judgment
  • Threshold tuning — the delta rule is a governed parameter reviewed against real alert performance, not a vendor default
The alert-burden discipline applies here too: a trend rule set too sensitive will fire on every post-sedation dip and be dismissed within a month, at which point the real decline gets dismissed with it. The delta thresholds belong in scheduled governance review with the cell therapy team, measured against a real false-positive rate — the same discipline that governs every other clinical alert in the building.

06 — Why this project

Clinical value and financial value are the same sentence here

Most healthcare IT projects have to choose which case to make: the clinical one or the financial one. This one doesn't, and that is the reason to build it. Catching neurotoxicity sixteen hours earlier means a patient who never loses the ability to write their name — and it means a bed that opens for the next person waiting for a therapy that is currently out of capacity. The same intervention, the same math, no tradeoff to negotiate. Projects that sit on that line are the ones that get funded, and more importantly, they are the ones worth building.

Demonstrated here

  • Clinical domain depth — CAR-T neurotoxicity, ICE scoring, grading, and the ICANS onset window
  • Trend detection over threshold logic — encoding how clinicians actually reason about deterioration
  • Escalation design — confirmation-first routing that protects alert credibility
  • Capacity-aware financial modeling — bed-days converted to treatment slots in a constrained service line
  • Byproduct compliance — structured capture that satisfies program reporting obligations automatically

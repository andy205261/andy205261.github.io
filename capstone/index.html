From Reactive to Predictive

A lifecycle strategy for the workstations on wheels that nurses use to scan every medication. Hospitals replace these carts by age, but their parts don't wear out at the same rate. The result: good chassis get thrown away while dead batteries stay on the floor, and when a scanner fails mid–medication pass, nurses work around the safety check. This capstone argues for replacing on condition instead of age — and shows it can pay for itself without new capital, new vendors, or new staff.

The 30-second version
1

The cart is where medication safety happens

Nurses scan the patient's wristband and the medication at a workstation on wheels. That scan is how hospitals meet the Joint Commission's two-identifier rule.

2

Its parts wear out on different clocks

Batteries last 3–5 years. The chassis lasts 7–10. Replacing the whole cart at one age means you're always too late for one part and too early for the other.

3

A failed scanner becomes a workaround

When the scan fails, nurses find another way to give the dose. Research ties those workarounds to roughly three times the odds of a medication error.

4

The fix changes the trigger, not the budget

Repairs are already covered by the vendor service contract. Sending that service to carts that are about to fail, instead of ones that already have, costs no new capital.

01 — The problem

One replacement age, two very different lifespans

The whole argument rests on this picture. A uniform replacement cycle of 5–6 years lands after the battery has already failed and before the chassis is worn out. It isn't a lack of diligence — calendar age is simply the only variable a fleet manager has when nobody is measuring condition. It's the wrong variable.

04 — Options weighed

Three alternatives, and why they lost

A recommendation is only as credible as the options it beat. Each of these is something a reasonable manager would propose first.

Ruled out

Shorten the age cycle to 3–4 years

Keeps pace with the battery, but retires chassis with years of life left — more cost, more e-waste, and it still schedules by the wrong variable. It treats the symptom, not the cause.

Ruled out

Bring component repair in-house

Requires new skills, spare-parts inventory, and likely forfeits existing warranty coverage. It turns a simple adjustment into an expensive program with a long payback.

Ruled out

Accept the failure rate as normal business risk

The current approach discards serviceable equipment while tolerating avoidable failures. Doing nothing forgoes an improvement that needs no investment.

Recommended

Replace on measured condition, using the service already paid for

Change what triggers a service call — predicted failure instead of actual failure — inside the existing vendor relationship. No new vendor spend, no insourcing, no new nursing procedures. Approve in principle now; confirm the numbers through SHC's internal data process.

05 — People and ethics

The parts a purely technical plan would miss

Ethical conditions

  • Knowledge creates duty. Once telemetry shows a cart is failing, keeping it in service is a choice, not an oversight. A written response-threshold policy must exist before monitoring is switched on.
  • Just culture. Workarounds are visible; the failing equipment behind them often isn't. Scan-rate data must never be used to blame nurses without checking the device first.
  • Fair distribution. Prioritizing high-acuity units is defensible, but it improves reliability unevenly — so the criteria should be transparent to nursing leadership, not buried in an algorithm.
  • Data governance. Report reliability by unit, never by individual clinician, and set that limit before the dashboard is designed.

Workforce and leadership

  • Reliability is a retention issue. 41% of nurses planning to leave within five years cite stress and burnout. A failing scanner during the med pass is a small, daily contributor.
  • About 30% of SHC staff are unionized, so any change to the medication-pass environment goes through labor partners and nursing shared governance, not an announcement.
  • Adoption fails on trust, not technology. The plan briefs councils first, recruits unit champions, and pilots visibly before asking anyone to scale.
  • The vendor is a partner, not a supplier. The key change can't happen through internal leadership alone — it has to be negotiated.
06 — Implementation

Three phases, each gated by the one before

Phasing lets SHC test the model's assumptions against its own data before committing at scale — which answers the report's biggest limitation directly.

1
Foundation · no capital

Fix the data, engage the vendor

Clean up device and configuration records so condition can be tied to a specific cart and location — also a HIPAA device-accountability requirement. Open vendor talks. Draft the response-threshold policy.

Success measureData accuracy threshold met; vendor agreement reviewed
2
Pilot

Monitor a mixed-acuity segment

Deploy condition monitoring on a limited slice of the fleet, including high- and low-acuity units, to generate SHC's own failure data and test the threshold policy under real conditions.

Success measureSHC-specific failure data generated; threshold policy tested
3
Scale · contingent on pilot

Enterprise rollout with tiered targets

Roll out fleet-wide with service-life targets set by utilization band, using what the pilot learned rather than a single uniform number.

Success measureFewer bedside-discovered failures against a confirmed baseline
07 — Why this is here

The business half of the same problem

The five projects on this portfolio are about what happens when clinical technology fails a patient. This capstone asks the question that comes before any of them get built: how do you get an organization to fund the fix? It's written for the people who approve budgets — and it argues from their constraints, not the analyst's enthusiasm.

What it demonstrates

  • Capital strategy under constraint — designing a solution to win against mandatory construction for scarce capital
  • Financial modeling with honest limits — a transparent model built from public data, with SHC-specific figures left as named data requests
  • Root-cause framing — the problem is the scheduling variable, not the equipment or the staff
  • Stakeholder reconciliation — finance wants predictability, nursing wants fewer failures; the plan gives both
  • Ethics as design requirements — policies that must exist before the technology switches on

What I'd say in an interview

  • "Age is the only variable a fleet manager has when nobody measures condition — so the fix is measuring condition, not buying carts sooner"
  • "I built the case so it didn't need a patient-harm number to pass. Harm avoided is upside, not the argument"
  • "At Stanford, a proposal that needs new capital is competing with seismic construction. Capital-neutral wasn't a nice-to-have, it was the entry ticket"
  • "The day you can see a cart failing, keeping it on the floor becomes a decision someone owns"
